### Big Idea 1: Worker-Owned Credentials Are the Structural Fix the Industry Keeps Avoiding

**The position**: The compliance model is broken not because companies lack good intentions, but because its architecture is wrong. Worker data is recreated from scratch every time someone crosses a site boundary, trapped in employer databases, and manually re-verified endlessly. The fix isn't a better HR system — it's a different model: credentials that belong to the worker, travel with them, and are verified once and reused everywhere.

**Why Matt owns it**: He built one of the only worker-owned credential systems in the category and has watched 130,000+ workers interact with a model where they — not the employer — hold the credential. He's not advocating a principle. He's describing something he proved at industrial scale.

**The analogy that matters**: Before open banking, financial data was locked inside your bank. You couldn't take it anywhere or let anyone else verify it efficiently. Open banking didn't just improve UX — it restructured power. Worker-owned credentials do the same thing for the industrial labour market.

**The harder truth**: The industrial workforce is now permanently contingent. FIFO workers move between operators. Contractors cycle across projects. Skilled tradespeople serve multiple clients simultaneously. The old assumption — that a worker stays put long enough for their employer to manage their credentials — no longer holds. The entire compliance infrastructure of mining, energy, and construction is still built on that assumption. The result: workers waste weeks re-proving qualifications they've already verified. Employers pay for compliance overhead that adds zero value. And the industry quietly accepts a fraud rate — one in 650 workers carrying a fake certificate — that would be scandalous in any other regulated sector.

**Proof points**:
- MyPass platform architecture: workers maintain a persistent profile; credentials travel with them; data reused across employers; onboarding friction decreases measurably
- 12 years of iteration on a single problem, not theoretical commentary
- Enterprise validation from operators (BHP, Woodside, Shell) that don't adopt new compliance infrastructure unless it genuinely works

**The investor/enterprise reframe**: This changes three conversations simultaneously — the investor conversation (platform economics, not SaaS multiples), the partnership conversation (infrastructure play, not vendor relationship), and the enterprise sales conversation (ecosystem access, not point solution).

### Big Idea 3: Safety Is a Systems-Design Problem, Not a Rules Problem

**The position**: Safety outcomes in high-risk industries aren't determined by the quality of inductions or the strictness of rules. They're determined by whether the system architecture ensures only the right, properly qualified people can ever end up in high-risk environments in the first place. The documentation of compliance and the assurance of compliance are two entirely different things — and most companies have only ever built the first.

**Why Matt owns it**: He spent years at Programmed personally accountable for moving large numbers of workers into hazardous sites, relying on spreadsheets and manual checks. He saw exactly how brittle those processes were. Then he spent a decade rebuilding that process from the ground up — not improving the paperwork, but redesigning the system.

**The regulatory reality**: Under WHS law, Accountable Officers now carry personal liability for contractor workforce readiness. Boards carry duty-of-care obligations that require evidenced real-time control. The compliance infrastructure most companies are running — siloed, unverified, non-interoperable — was never designed to produce that evidence. You can't evidence what you haven't standardised. And you can't standardise what every operator, contractor, and training provider is managing differently in their own silo. The regulatory clock has moved. The infrastructure hasn't. That gap is where incidents happen and prosecutions land.

**Proof points**:
- Lived the failure modes of traditional safety workflows at Programmed
- Built MyPass specifically to hard-wire competency, compliance, and access control into how work is organised
- BHP, Woodside, Shell trust MyPass to manage worker credentials at scale — operational proof, not theoretical

**The position**: Most buyers frame workforce compliance as a tick-box cost — HSE overhead, admin burden. The real thesis is that verified competency is a competitive advantage: faster mobilisation, lower insurance risk, cleaner wins on tender. The question isn't "how much does this cost?" It's "what is it costing you not to have it?"

**Why Matt owns it**: He has the data no one else has. Platform-derived metrics from 1,500+ organisations running verified workforce compliance at scale — 3x faster mobilisation, 27% reduction in non-compliant access, 32% reduction in admin time, 100 hours saved per compliance cycle. No consultant or competitor has this dataset. The reframe from cost to advantage is credible because he can show the P&L impact, not just argue the logic.

**The commercial reframe**: Verified workforces win more tenders. Service providers using MyPass can bid for work that requires evidenced workforce readiness. That's a revenue argument — and one Matt can substantiate with customer outcomes, not just product claims.

**Full-stack visibility**: Most safety tech vendors talk to HSE managers. Most procurement reformers talk to CPOs. Matt's platform connects operators, contractors, and workers simultaneously. He sees the cost from every angle in the supply chain — and that's what gives him standing to reframe the whole equation, not just one stakeholder's version of it.

### Big Idea 5: Trust in Industrial Ecosystems Should Be Structural, Not Relational

**The position**: The resources and energy industry has spent a decade trying to fix broken contractor relationships through better contracts, reformed procurement, and stronger relationship management. None of it has worked at scale — because the root cause isn't relational. It's information asymmetry. Operators can't trust what they can't verify. Contractors can't perform to a standard that was never defined consistently. Workers carry the cost of proving themselves from scratch every time they cross a site boundary. The fix isn't a better contract. It's a shared layer of verified truth that every party in the ecosystem can see and trust simultaneously — so that trust stops being something you build over years and becomes something the infrastructure provides by default.

**Why Matt owns it**: Every other voice in this conversation — consultants, procurement reformers, industry bodies — is diagnosing the problem from the outside and prescribing behavioural change. Matt is the only person who built the technical infrastructure that makes the root cause solvable. He's already deployed the verified data layer — across 1,500 organisations and 130,000+ workers — that removes the information deficit underpinning every broken contractor relationship.

## Contrarian Beliefs (Things Most People in the Industry Won't Say Publicly)

- **The fraud rate is an industry-wide admission of failure.** One in 650 workers carrying a fake certificate isn't a bad-actor problem. It's what happens when the verification system is so fragmented and manual that fraud becomes easy. Every organisation that keeps running manual checks is implicitly accepting this rate.

- **Most compliance programs don't produce compliance.** They produce documentation of intent. The distinction matters enormously when a regulator or a coroner asks what real-time control looked like on the day of an incident.

- **Operators are paying for compliance overhead that creates zero safety value.** The cost of re-verifying credentials that have already been verified elsewhere doesn't make anyone safer. It makes administrators busier and auditors satisfied. Those are different things.

- **Worker privacy in industrial compliance is treated as a nuisance.** The industry collects enormous amounts of worker data and treats data ownership as an employer prerogative. That model is legally fragile and operationally counterproductive. When workers own their credentials, the whole system gets faster, not just more fair.

- **The regulatory framework has already moved. Most companies haven't noticed yet.** Personal liability for AOs under WHS law isn't a future risk — it's current exposure for anyone running a non-evidenced contractor compliance program right now.
